Part 1 · How it works

It writes one word at a time

You type

“The sun rises in the”

AI weighs the next word

east94%
morning4%
sky2%

Numbers are illustrative.

  • It has read an enormous amount of books, articles, and websites
  • It learns patterns, the way you know “once upon a” ends with “time”
  • It predicts word by word, so fast it seems instant
  • Not magic: very sophisticated pattern matching. That’s why it can sound right and still be wrong.
Understanding and Using AI

Here is the one technical idea for today. If I say “the sun rises in the,” you all finish it: east. You’ve heard that sentence so many times that the next word is obvious. AI works the same way. It has read so much that it can predict what word should come next, then the next, then the next, until it has written a whole answer.

Notice the last point. It predicts what sounds right. Most of the time, what sounds right is right. Sometimes it isn’t, and the answer sounds just as confident either way. That’s the most important thing to understand about these tools.

Part 1 · Limitations

Where AI still stumbles

  • It can sound sure and still be wrong. It sometimes invents facts, quotes, and even sources.
  • It tends to agree with you. Ask it what’s wrong with your idea, and it will tell you.
  • It only knows your situation if you tell it, so be thoughtful about what you share.
  • It isn’t your doctor, lawyer, or financial advisor. It can help you prepare good questions for them.

In a study by the BBC and European public broadcasters, 45% of AI answers about the news had at least one significant error.

Understanding and Using AI

Now the honest part. Because it predicts what sounds right, it can be confidently wrong. The technical word is “hallucination.” It has invented book titles, court cases, and quotes. Courts are still fining lawyers this year for filing cases that AI made up. So anything that matters, you check.

The study at the bottom is from late 2025: public broadcasters, including the BBC, asked AI assistants about the news, and almost half the answers had a significant error. It’s getting better. It isn’t there yet.

Second, these tools are built to be agreeable. If you want real feedback, ask for it: “What are the weaknesses of this plan?”

Third, it doesn’t know you. It only knows what you tell it, which is a reason to give it context and also a reason to be careful with private information.

And fourth, it doesn’t replace professionals. I use it before a doctor’s appointment to understand terms and write down my questions. Then I ask the doctor.

Ask better, get better

Part 5 · Staying safe

Scams got smarter, too

What’s new

  • Cloned voices: a call that sounds exactly like your grandchild, in trouble and needing money
  • Fake videos of celebrities and officials selling investments
  • Polished emails and texts, with no typos to give them away

What to do

  • Hang up and call back on a number you know
  • Choose a family safe word, as the FBI advises
  • Slow down. Urgency, secrecy, gift cards, crypto, or wire transfers mean scam.

In 2025, Americans 60 and older reported losing $7.7 billion to scams, up 59% in one year. (FBI)

Understanding and Using AI

This is the most important slide today, so I’m going to slow down.

The same technology that writes your thank-you note can copy a voice from a few seconds of video online. The classic version: a call that sounds exactly like your grandchild, crying, saying they’ve been in an accident or arrested and need money right away, and please don’t tell Mom. There are also fake videos of celebrities and news anchors selling investments, and phishing emails that no longer have the spelling mistakes we used to look for.

The FBI’s numbers for 2025: Americans sixty and older reported losing seven point seven billion dollars to scams, up fifty-nine percent in one year. The FBI also says many victims of AI-powered scams never realize AI was involved, so the real number is higher.

Three defenses. Hang up and call the person back on a number you already have. Agree on a family safe word, a word only your family knows, and ask for it. And slow down: urgency, secrecy, and unusual payment like gift cards, crypto, or wire transfers are the signature of a scam.

Ask the room: has anyone gotten a call or a text like this? Then: tonight, pick your family safe word. It’s on the homework list.

To report a scam: ReportFraud.ftc.gov or ic3.gov. Both are on the participant page.
