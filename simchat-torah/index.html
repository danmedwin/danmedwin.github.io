Simchat Torah 5787

The Unrolled Torah

Twenty stops to point out as you run the inside of the circle, in the order they appear on the parchment. Each one has where to find it, what to look for, something to say, and photos from a real Torah scroll.

Running the circle

  • Start at the rollers. When the whole scroll is open, the end of D’varim meets the beginning of B’reishit.
  • Keep the parchment on your right and you are moving forward through the Torah, from B’reishit toward D’varim. (This assumes the writing faces into the circle.)
  • Book boundaries are four blank lines, the gaps in the ring. Use them as mile markers.
  • Halfway around is the middle of the Torah, in Vayikra.
  • Gold pins are the ten must-sees if you’re short on time. Tap a pin to jump to it.

Percentages come from counting the letters of the Masoretic text, so they hold for any scroll. Column numbers follow the standard 245-column layout; if almost every column in your scroll begins with ו, it very likely uses that layout.

בראשית B'reishit Genesis

1

In the Beginning

Genesis 1:1–2:3 · בראשית

Must-see

Where The very first column and a half, right beside the rollers. 0% around column 1

Look for An oversized ב opens the Torah. Then count the paragraphs: each day of Creation ends with the rest of its line left blank, so the start of the Torah reads like a list of seven days.

Say The Torah begins with bet, the second letter. The rabbis noticed that ב is closed on the top, the bottom, and the back, and open only toward the words ahead. Their lesson: start here and keep moving forward (B’reishit Rabbah 1:10).

Bonus Just after the seventh day (Genesis 2:4), the word בהבראם (b’hibar’am, “when they were created”) has a tiny ה. The Talmud says this world was created with the letter hei (Talmud Menachot 29b).

2

Esau’s Kiss

Genesis 33:4 · וישקהו

Where A little past the middle of B’reishit. 16% around column 38

Look for Six dots floating over one word: וישקהו (vayishakeihu, “and he kissed him”).

Say After twenty years apart, Esau runs to hug and kiss his twin brother, Jacob. Did he mean it? Some rabbis read the dots as a hint that he didn’t. Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai said that in that moment, Esau kissed Jacob with his whole heart (Rashi on Genesis 33:4). Ask: what do you think the dots mean?

Bonus The Torah has dotted words in ten places. Four more are in B’reishit: Genesis 16:5, 18:9, 19:33, and 37:12.

3

Y’hudah at the Top

Genesis 49:8 · יהודה

Where Near the end of B’reishit, about two columns before the first four-line gap. 25% around column 59

Look for In many scrolls, a column starts with the name יהודה (Y’hudah, Judah) instead of the usual ו.

Say Jacob is blessing his sons, and he tells Judah that his brothers will praise him. Judah’s name comes from the Hebrew for praise and thanks (Genesis 29:35), and it gives us the word Y’hudi: Jew.

Four blank lines. B'reishit ends and Sh'mot begins. 26% around column 61

שמות Sh'mot Exodus

4

The Song of the Sea

Exodus 14:28–15:21 · שירת הים

Must-seeVisible across the circle

Where About a third of the way into Sh’mot. 33% around column 78

Look for A whole column that looks like a brick wall: short phrases stacked over blank spaces. In many scrolls the column begins with the word הבאים (haba’im).

Say The Israelites have just crossed the sea on dry ground, with walls of water on both sides. Moses and the people sing, then Miriam leads the women with her timbrel. The song is written like a wall of bricks; the Talmud calls the pattern “half-brick over whole brick” (Talmud Megillah 16b). Find מי כמכה (Mi chamochah) inside it. We sing it at services all the time.

5

The Ten Commandments

Exodus 20:1–14 · עשרת הדברות

Must-see

Where About five columns after the Song of the Sea. 35% around columns 83–84

Look for A line of tiny two-word phrases with gaps between them: לא תרצח · לא תנאף · לא תגנב (lo tirtzach, lo tinaf, lo tignov).

Say Some commandments are only two words long, and each one still gets its own space on the parchment. Ask: how many of the ten can you name?

6

God’s Big Kindness

Exodus 34:6–14 · נצר חסד

Where About four-fifths of the way through Sh’mot. 42% around columns 101–102

Look for A big נ at the start of נצר חסד (notzeir chesed, “keeping kindness”). A few lines on, a column often begins with שמר (sh’mor), and just below that the ר of אחר (acheir, “another”) is big too.

Say You heard these words again and again on Yom Kippur: Adonai, Adonai, El rachum v’chanun. God keeps kindness for thousands of generations, and the letter is big. The big ר makes sure no one misreads acheir (“another” god) as echad (“One”).

Four blank lines. Sh'mot ends and Vayikra begins. 46% around column 111

ויקרא Vayikra Leviticus

7

The Tiny Alef

Leviticus 1:1 · ויקרא

Must-see

Where The very first word after the second four-line gap. 46% around column 111

Look for The last letter of ויקרא (Vayikra, “called”), the first word of the book, is a small א. How small depends on the scribe. In some scrolls it is tiny; in the scroll in these photos, it is only a bit smaller than the א of אל right beside it.

Say The Torah calls Moses the humblest person on earth (Numbers 12:3). One tradition says Moses wanted to leave off the alef, so the word would say God only happened to meet him. When God insisted, he wrote it small (Baal HaTurim on Leviticus 1:1).

8

The Middle of the Torah

Leviticus 10:16 · 11:42 · 13:33 · גחון

Must-see

Where About a third of the way into Vayikra, almost directly across the circle from the rollers. 52% around columns 122–126

Look for Three middles in a row: דרש דרש (darosh darash), the middle words; a big ו in גחון (gachon), the middle letter; and a big ג in והתגלח (v’hitgalach), the middle verse (Talmud Kiddushin 30a). In the scroll pictured here, the first דרש ends one line and the second begins the next.

Say Gachon means belly, as in creatures that crawl on their bellies. So the middle letter of the Torah sits in the word for belly. Welcome to the Torah’s belly button!

Bonus When computers count every letter, the exact middle lands a few chapters earlier, in Leviticus 8. The Talmud itself admits the rabbis weren’t sure of every spelling (Talmud Kiddushin 30a).

9

Love Your Neighbor

Leviticus 19:18 · ואהבת לרעך כמוך

Big idea, no special look

Where About two-thirds of the way through Vayikra. 56% around column 135

Look for No special look here. Point to ואהבת לרעך כמוך (v’ahavta l’rei’acha kamocha), love your neighbor as yourself.

Say Rabbi Akiva called this the great principle of the whole Torah (Sifra, K’doshim 4:12). It lives just past the Torah’s middle.

10

The Holiday We’re Celebrating

Leviticus 23:33–43 · בסכת תשבו

Big idea, no special look

Where About four-fifths of the way through Vayikra. 58% around columns 141–142

Look for No special look here. Point to בסכת תשבו שבעת ימים (basukkot teishvu shivat yamim), live in sukkot for seven days (23:42). Nearby: the lulav and etrog (23:40) and the eighth day, Sh’mini Atzeret (23:36).

Say This paragraph is why we have Sukkot and Sh’mini Atzeret. Finishing the Torah and starting it again with a party came later, from the Jewish people. That party is Simchat Torah!

Four blank lines. Vayikra ends and B'midbar begins. 61% around column 148

במדבר B'midbar Numbers

11

The Priestly Blessing

Numbers 6:24–26 · ברכת כהנים

Where About a fifth of the way into B’midbar. 65% around column 158

Look for Three short blessings, each followed by a gap, so they stand apart on the parchment. The first begins יברכך (Y’varech’cha Adonai v’yishm’recha, May God bless you and protect you).

Say Three verses that grow: three words, then five, then seven. Many families bless their children with these words on Friday night.

Bonus Keep walking into Numbers 7: nearly the same paragraph repeats twelve times, once for each tribe’s leader. We read it on Chanukah.

12

The Backward Nuns

Numbers 10:35–36 · ויהי בנסע

Must-see

Where About a third of the way into B’midbar. 68% around column 165

Look for Two backward or upside-down נ marks, one before and one after a two-verse paragraph that begins ויהי בנסע הארן (Vay’hi binso’a ha’aron).

Say Many of us sing these words as the Torah comes out of the ark. The Talmud says the marks set this little passage apart as a book of its own, which would make the Torah seven books instead of five (Talmud Shabbat 116a).

Bonus Four chapters later (Numbers 14:17), the smallest letter gets big: a large י in יגדל (yigdal, “let it be great”), as Moses asks God to forgive the people.

13

Mah Tovu at the Top

Numbers 24:5 · מה טבו

Must-see

Where About two-thirds of the way through B’midbar. 75% around column 184

Look for In many scrolls, a column begins with מה טבו (Mah tovu) instead of the usual ו.

Say We sing these words at the start of morning services. They were first said by Balaam, a prophet hired to curse the Israelites, who blessed them instead.

14

The Broken Vav

Numbers 25:11–12 · שלום

Where About one column after Mah Tovu. 76% around column 185

Look for A ו with a crack across its middle, in the word שלום (shalom, peace). Just above it, the י in פינחס (Pinchas) is small. The crack can be a hairline, so get close.

Say After an act of violence, God gives Pinchas b’riti shalom, a covenant of peace, and the vav in shalom is broken. Many teachers read it this way: peace that comes from violence isn’t whole. Ask: what do you think?

15

Five Sisters, One Big Letter

Numbers 27:1–11 · משפטן

Where About three-quarters of the way through B’midbar, a few columns after the broken vav. 77% around column 188

Look for A big final ן at the end of משפטן (mishpatan, their case).

Say Machlah, No’ah, Choglah, Milkah, and Tirtzah had no brothers, so their father’s land would have been lost. They stood up in front of everyone and asked for their fair share. Moses brought their case to God, and God said they were right. The big letter is the ending that means “their,” as in these five women’s.

Four blank lines. B'midbar ends and D'varim begins. 82% around column 200

דברים D'varim Deuteronomy

16

The Ten Commandments, Take Two

Deuteronomy 5:6–18 · עשרת הדברות

Where About a fifth of the way into D’varim. 85% around columns 208–209

Look for The same gapped line of short commandments, now joined by ו (and): ולא תנאף, ולא תגנב.

Say Moses retells the Ten Commandments with a few changes. In Exodus we zachor (remember) Shabbat; here we shamor (keep) it. That’s why we sing shamor v’zachor b’dibur echad in L’cha Dodi.

17

The Sh’ma

Deuteronomy 6:4–9 · שמע

Must-see

Where A column or two after the Ten Commandments. 86% around column 210

Look for A big ע at the end of שמע (Sh’ma) and a big ד at the end of אחד (echad). The V’ahavta follows right after.

Say Put the two big letters together and they spell עד (eid), witness. Every time we say the Sh’ma, we are witnesses (Baal HaTurim on Deuteronomy 6:4).

18

Secret Dots

Deuteronomy 29:27–28 · לנו ולבנינו

Where Six or seven columns before the end. 97% around column 239

Look for A big ל in וישלכם (vayashlicheim). In the next verse, dots over לנו ולבנינו (lanu ul’vaneinu, for us and our children) and over the ע of עד (ad, forever).

Say The verse says the hidden things belong to God, and the things we can see belong to us and our children forever. It’s the last of the Torah’s ten dotted places.

19

Ha’azinu

Deuteronomy 32:1–43 · האזינו

Must-seeVisible across the circle

Where The last few columns. In many scrolls, the column where the song starts begins with ואעידה (v’a’idah). 99% around columns 242–243

Look for A song written in two neat columns, like a poem. In verse 6, a big ה stands alone as its own word.

Say This is Moses’s last song, calling heaven and earth to listen: Ha’azinu hashamayim. It’s easy to spot from across the circle.

20

The Last Line

Deuteronomy 33–34 · לעיני כל ישראל

Must-see

Where The very end, right beside the rollers where you started. 100% around column 245

Look for Scribes plan the whole scroll so the Torah ends in the middle of the last line of the last column (Mishneh Torah, Laws of Tefillin, Mezuzah, and the Torah Scroll 7:7). The final words, לעיני כל ישראל (l’einei kol Yisrael, before the eyes of all Israel), end partway across, and the rest of the line stays blank.

Say The last letter of the Torah is ל. Step over the rollers to the first letter, ב. Together they spell לב (lev), heart. We finish, and we start again right away.

Bonus A column or two back, Deuteronomy 33:4: תורה צוה לנו משה (Torah tzivah lanu Moshe), the verse the Talmud says children should learn first (Talmud Sukkah 42a).

Anywhere you stop

Things to point out on any column while you run between stops.

  • Crowns Seven letters, ש ע ט נ ז ג צ, wear three tiny crowns called tagin. The Talmud imagines Moses watching God tie them on, then visiting Rabbi Akiva’s classroom far in the future and not following the lesson (Talmud Menachot 29b).
  • Column tops In many scrolls, almost every column begins with ו. The style is called Vavei HaAmudim, “hooks of the pillars” (Exodus 27:10). Six columns open with fixed words whose first letters spell ביה שמו (Psalm 68:5): B’reishit, Y’hudah, Haba’im, Sh’mor, Mah Tovu, and V’a’idah. All six are on this tour.
  • No vowels No vowels, no trope marks, no verse numbers, no punctuation. Whoever reads from the scroll prepares all of that ahead of time.
  • Two kinds of breaks A p’tuchah (open break) leaves the rest of a line blank. A s’tumah (closed break) leaves a gap in the middle of a line. They are the Torah’s paragraphs.
  • Seams Sheets of parchment are sewn together with sinew (gid). Find a seam and count how many columns fit on one sheet.
  • Forty-two lines Count the lines in any column. Many scrolls have forty-two in every one.
  • Stretched letters To end each line exactly at the margin, the sofer (scribe) stretches a few letters extra wide. Look for a long, flat letter at the end of a line.
  • God’s name Before writing God’s name, the sofer says it is being written for its holiness. A mistake in the Name can’t be erased.
